# Why a stock falls more than 10% in a week

A drop of more than 10% in a week is a repricing event. Classify the cause as **temporary** or **permanent**.

- **Temporary:** the cash-generating business is unchanged; only the price or the current quarter is worse.
- **Permanent:** earnings power is lower, or common equity’s claim on it is smaller, and that will not reverse with the tape.

Test: if cash generation and the claim on it are intact, the drop is temporary. If either is permanently worse, it is not.

## Temporary

1. **Forced selling** — holders must sell because of margin calls, fund redemptions, ETF outflows, or risk limits. The company’s customers, margins, and cash generation are unchanged.
2. **Index and factor flows** — the stock is sold because it left an index, a factor (momentum, growth, quality) reversed, or a basket was rebalanced. Nothing company-specific changed in the business.
3. **Crowded positioning unwind** — too many funds were long the same name; they exit together. Price falls from positioning, not from a change in long-term cash flows.
4. **Macro multiple compression** — rates, recession fear, or market-wide risk-off lower the P/E. This company’s revenue, costs, and competitive position are not the reason.
5. **Timing miss** — a shipment, contract, or restock slips by a quarter, or weather/destocking hits one period. The order book and run-rate earnings power are intact.
6. **Sandbagged guidance** — management cuts the outlook to be conservative after a scare. The cut is a communication choice, not evidence that demand or margins have a new, lower run-rate.
7. **Non-cash accounting noise** — a restatement, impairment, or delayed filing that does not change cash earned or cash that will be earned. Solvency and operations are intact.
8. **Temporary cost spike** — energy, freight, or a commodity jumps and is expected to mean-revert or be passed through. Unit economics are not permanently worse.
9. **Peer contagion** — a competitor or the sector is sold after someone else’s failure. This firm’s customers, contracts, and balance sheet are not the failed firm.
10. **False or overstated headline** — rumor, short-seller report, or product scare that is later disproved or bounded. No lasting loss of franchise, cash, or legal standing.

## Permanent

1. **Demand is gone** — the product, category, or use-case is obsolete or in secular decline. Volume will not return to the old path even if the market rallies.
2. **Competition destroyed the position** — a rival has a better or cheaper offer, or a platform disintermediates the company. Pricing power and share are lost for good.
3. **Unit economics are broken** — costs (wages, inputs, regulation) are structurally higher and cannot be passed through. The business no longer earns an adequate return on capital.
4. **Balance sheet is broken** — debt is too large versus cash generation; maturities cannot be refinanced except on punitive terms. Survival depends on dilution or restructuring.
5. **Fraud or fake earnings** — reported revenue, inventory, or profits were not real. Past earnings power was overstated; the true machine is weaker or insolvent.
6. **Franchise revoked by law** — a ban, lost license, breakup, or a fine that is a new ongoing cost of doing business. The old legal right to operate or price is gone.
7. **Key customer or contract is gone** — a concentrated relationship ended and will not be replaced at the same scale or margin. The “moat” was that relationship.
8. **Capital allocation or governance turned extractive** — empire-building, related-party leakage, or a roll-up that only worked with cheap paper. Cash that belonged to owners is being destroyed.
9. **Industry structure shifted** — overcapacity, commoditization, or a new technical standard that strands the installed base. Excess supply or the new standard will last years, not a quarter.
10. **Common equity’s claim was reduced** — covenants, preferreds, a distressed exchange, or heavy dilution permanently shrink what residual owners get even if the firm survives.
